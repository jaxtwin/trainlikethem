Notes/02.09.26/Editorial

Nobody stays in Thor shape

Twenty-two of the thirty-five programs here were built for a release date. The bodies they produced were never meant to last, and almost nobody publishes what came after.

2 Sept 20266 min readBuilt from 35 documented programs

Read the kickers on this site and a pattern shows up fast. Role prep. Fight camp. Baywatch cut. Wonder Woman prep. Twenty-two of the thirty-five programs here belong to actors, and most of those were built backwards from a date somebody else picked — the first day of principal photography, or a poster shoot six weeks before it.

That changes what the program is. It isn't a way of training. It's a countdown.

The programs say so themselves

You can see it in the numbers without reading a single interview. The mass blocks carry calorie figures — 4,500, 5,000 — that only make sense attached to a specific bodyweight, a specific training volume and an end date. The cuts run the other way, 2,000 or 2,200 alongside five or six training days, which is a deficit nobody sustains for fun.

Neither figure describes a life. Both describe a push toward a deadline, run under supervision, with a kitchen and a schedule arranged around it.

The part nobody prints

Here is what's actually missing from all thirty-five: a maintenance phase. Not one of these programs documents what the person did after the film wrapped or the fight was over. The record simply stops.

That's not an oversight on our part. It's what the sources do. A studio publicises the transformation because the transformation sells the film. Nobody publishes the eight months afterwards, because there's nothing to sell and the honest version — training dropped to three days a week, calories came down, some of the size went with them — makes a worse headline.

So we don't print it either, and this note exists to say that plainly rather than let the silence imply something false.

What a deadline does to a program

Once you know a program was built toward a date, its shape stops looking arbitrary. Volume is front-loaded because there's no reason to hold anything back for month four — there is no month four. Calories sit at whichever extreme the brief requires, because the cost of sitting there only has to be paid for a few weeks. Recovery gets bought rather than scheduled: massage, physio, sleep protected by someone else managing the calendar.

None of that is cheating. It's rational under the constraint. But every one of those decisions is only defensible because the thing ends, and none of them survive being made permanent.

The three groups behave differently

Split the thirty-five by who they belong to and the deadline problem sorts itself neatly. The twenty-two actors are working to release dates, so their programs are blocks with hard edges. The ten athletes work to a season, which means their year already contains an off-season, a build and a taper — the peaks are planned and so are the troughs. The three bodybuilders make the distinction explicit, because off-season and contest prep are separate disciplines with separate names.

Only one of those three groups pretends the peak is the normal state, and it's the group that got the most magazine coverage.

Why it matters if you're copying one

If you run a six-day mass block at 4,500 calories with no end date in mind, you are not running the program. You are running the first six weeks of it forever, which is a different thing and a worse one.

The programs that hold up outside a deadline are the ones that weren't built for one. The athletes' weeks are shaped around a season, so they assume peaks and troughs by design. The self-programmed lifters — five of the thirty-five — tend to run something they've done for years, which is its own kind of evidence.

And the two bodybuilders in the set are the clearest case of all: their whole sport is built on the distinction between off-season and contest prep. They'd never confuse one for the other. It's the rest of us, reading a magazine feature about a six-week transformation, who do.

What to take from it

Use the peaks as reference, not as targets. If you want to know what a hard week looks like when someone is paid to train, the seven full write-ups will tell you in detail. If you want something you can still be running next year, look at the days-per-week column first and pick something you'd do in a bad month, not a good one.

The bodies on this site are real and the programs that made them are documented. What isn't documented is anybody keeping them.

What the honest version would look like

If somebody did publish the after, it would probably read as an anticlimax. Training down to three or four days. Calories somewhere near maintenance. Some of the size gone within a few months, most of the strength kept for longer, the shape softening in ways that photograph badly and matter not at all.

That's not failure. That's what happens when a temporary project ends and a person goes back to having a life. The reason it never gets written up is that there's no product attached to it.

One thing we can say from the record

The programs on this site that look most like something a person could keep doing are, almost without exception, the ones that were never built for a camera — the self-programmed weeks and the in-season athletic blocks. They tend to be lower in volume, steadier in calories, and duller to read.

Duller to read and easier to still be doing in two years. That trade is the actual finding, and it's available in the data without anybody having to confess anything.
