The five exercises that keep showing up

Seven programs on this site are written out session by session. Count what's in them and the overlap isn't the lifts you'd expect.

19 Aug 20263 min readBuilt from 7 full write-ups

Only seven of the thirty-five programs here are documented at the level of individual sessions. That's a small sample and it skews toward the mass and strength blocks, so treat what follows as a description of those seven rather than a law of training.

Still, when you count every named movement across every session in those seven weeks, the same handful keeps reappearing:

Leg curl5
Romanian deadlift4
Lateral raise4
Barbell curl4
Leg press3
Standing calf raise3

The squat and bench aren't at the top

Back squat, deadlift, overhead press, weighted pull-up and close-grip bench all appear, but twice each. The lifts that repeat most are the ones nobody writes headlines about: a hamstring curl, an RDL, a side delt raise, a curl.

There's a straightforward reason. The heavy compounds are the anchors, and an anchor appears once in a session by design — you don't squat three different ways in a week. The isolation work fills the space around it, and there's more space than there are anchors.

How this was counted

Every session in the seven detailed write-ups was read, every named movement extracted, and identical movements merged where the naming differed. Set and rep schemes were ignored — this counts appearances, not volume, so a lift programmed for three sets counts the same as one programmed for six.

That's a real limitation and it cuts a specific way: it flatters accessories. An exercise that appears in five sessions at two sets each looks more prominent here than a squat that appears twice at five heavy sets. Read the table as a map of what fills a training week, not of where the work is.

What the repeats have in common

Look at what those five actually do. Leg curl and RDL both cover the posterior chain, which is the group a squat-and-press program leaves underdone. Lateral raise builds the delt width that reads as a wider frame on camera. Barbell curl does the same for arms.

For programs built to change how a body looks on screen, that's not a coincidence. It's the whole brief. The compounds handle strength and mass; the isolation work handles shape, and shape is what gets someone cast.

What it says about how these were programmed

There's a structural insight buried in the counts. In all seven programs the heavy compound work is narrow and repeated — the same two or three anchors, week after week, with load as the variable. The accessory work is broad and rotated. Nobody is hunting for novel compound lifts; they're adding load to a short list and varying everything around it.

That's the opposite of how most self-written programs drift over time, which is toward more compound variations and a fixed accessory list. The documented set suggests the reverse: fix the anchors, vary the edges.

The lifts that appear once

Worth noting what showed up a single time across all seven weeks: front squat, good morning, farmer's carry, sled work, box jump. Each appears in exactly one program, usually the one whose sport demanded it.

Which is a reasonable argument against building a week from a list of exercises somebody famous did. The single-appearance lifts are there for reasons specific to one person's sport, injury history or filming schedule, and none of those reasons is yours.

If you took only these

A week built from an RDL, a leg curl, a lateral raise, a curl and a leg press would be a poor program — no vertical press, no horizontal push, no real pull. That's the wrong lesson to draw.

The useful lesson is the reverse. If your training is squat, bench, deadlift and rows and nothing else, you're missing exactly the movements these seven programs spend most of their volume on. Adding two of them changes more about how you look than adding another set of bench.
