The calorie number is not the program

The documented figures run from 2,000 to 5,500 a day. Copying the number without the context around it is the most common way these programs fail.

Across the thirty-five programs the reported intake spans 2,000 to 5,500 calories a day, with a median of 3,000. Both ends are real, documented figures. Neither is a recommendation.

Why the high numbers are high

A 4,500 or 5,000 calorie figure almost always comes with three things attached: a large body, a training week at five or six sessions, and a deadline. Role-prep mass blocks are short. They run for weeks, under supervision, with the explicit intention of ending. The calorie figure is a function of that person's bodyweight, their training volume, and how long they intend to keep it up.

Lift the number out of that context and it stops describing a program and starts describing a surplus.

The structure transfers. The absolute number does not.

Why the low numbers are low

The same applies at the other end. A 2,000 calorie cut alongside six training days is a deliberate, temporary, monitored state — usually aimed at a specific shooting date or weigh-in. It is not a way to live, and the people running it were not living that way either, before or after.

What transfers and what does not

The structure transfers: protein anchored to bodyweight, most carbohydrate placed around training, calories set against a goal and adjusted from actual weekly change. The absolute number does not. It was computed for someone else's mass, training load, and deadline.

This is why the diet page is organised by calorie band rather than by person. The band is the useful unit. The name attached to it is not.

The practical version

Start from your own bodyweight and training frequency, use the documented figures as a sense of the range these bodies operate in, and adjust from what the scale and the gym actually tell you over two or three weeks. That is slower than copying a number, and it is the only version that works.

None of this is medical or dietary advice. Programs here are documented, not prescribed.
