The only two supplements worth buying

Nine appear across the documented stacks. Two have both frequency and evidence behind them, and between them they cost very little.

5 Sept 20263 min readBuilt from 7 documented supplement stacks

Seven of the thirty-five programs here publish a supplement stack. Counted across those seven, two items appear in nearly all of them and also survive contact with the evidence.

Everything else in the set is either situational or a historical artefact of when the program was written.

What to look for rather than what to buy

This is deliberately not a brand roundup. We have appearance counts from documented programs, not independent testing, so naming a winner would be inventing a verdict. What follows is the category and the specification that matters — take it to whichever brand is cheapest per serving with third-party testing.

Some links below are affiliate links. They do not affect the verdicts — four of the nine supplements assessed on this site are marked skip, including the second most common one. Full disclosure.

Whey protein

Appears in 6 of 7 stacksLook for Third-party testedForm Concentrate or isolate

The most common item in the entire documented set, and the only one whose purpose is straightforwardly nutritional: it's food in powder form, useful because hitting protein targets from whole food alone is inconvenient at the intakes these programs run. Concentrate is cheaper and fine unless lactose is a problem.

Good
  • Solves a real problem — protein volume
  • Cheapest way to add 25 g of protein
  • No mechanism to be sceptical about
Less good
  • Unnecessary if you already hit protein from food
  • Quality varies and testing is uneven
  • Flavouring and fillers differ wildly

Creatine monohydrate

Appears in 5 of 7 stacksDose 3–5 g dailyForm Monohydrate only

The most studied supplement in sport and among the cheapest. Monohydrate is the form with the evidence behind it; the pricier variants exist to be different rather than better. Timing does not matter, consistency does.

Good
  • Best evidence base of anything in the set
  • Costs pennies per day
  • Works without loading or cycling
Less good
  • Effect is real but modest
  • Some water retention early on
  • Nothing happens quickly

These three appear rarely and for identifiable reasons — heat, cramping, sleep, latitude. Worth testing on yourself if one of those applies to you. Not worth taking by default, and not worth taking all three because a program you admire listed one.

Good
  • Cheap to trial
  • Clear use cases exist
  • Low downside
Less good
  • Easy to take pointlessly
  • D3 needs testing to dose sensibly
  • No effect if you weren't deficient

How we selected

Every supplement named across the 35 documented programs is counted on the gear page, with a verdict against each. This post takes the nine assessed items and reports the two that have both frequency in the documented set and an evidence base outside it.

Frequency alone is explicitly not the criterion, and the multivitamin is why: it appears in 12 of 35 programs, more than creatine, and we still say skip. Popularity in this sample measures what supplement stacks looked like when they were written.

We have not tested products and name no brands. Prices are per-serving estimates to show the order of magnitude.

Questions

Which creatine should I buy?
Any monohydrate with third-party testing. The form is the only thing that matters and it's the cheapest form.
Do I need whey if I eat enough protein?
No. It solves convenience, not deficiency. If you're hitting your target from food, you're done.
What about the multivitamin in so many programs?
It's the second most common item in the set and we still say skip — the reasoning is in the note on the four to skip.
Is any of this medical advice?
No. None of this is medical or dietary advice. Talk to a doctor before starting a supplement, particularly if you take medication.
