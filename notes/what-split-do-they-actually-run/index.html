What split do they actually run?

Across all thirty-five documented programs, the most common answer is not push/pull/legs. It is not even a named split.

Split arguments usually get settled by preference. Here is what thirty-five documented programs actually use, counted rather than argued.

Hybrid wins because the job is not bodybuilding

Eleven of thirty-five is the largest single group, and it is mostly explained by who is in the sample: twenty-two actors, ten athletes, three bodybuilders. An actor preparing for a role needs to look a specific way on a specific date while staying able to work twelve-hour shooting days. An in-season athlete lifts around a competition calendar. Neither job is served by a split optimised purely for hypertrophy.

The five bro splits are almost entirely the bodybuilders and the size-focused role blocks. The eight full-body programs skew toward the athletes and the conditioning-led weeks, where frequency matters more than per-session volume.

The split is the easy part to copy. The frequency is the part that quietly decides whether copying works.

Frequency is less varied than the splits

Twenty-nine of the thirty-five run five or six days a week — fifteen at six days, fourteen at five. Five run four days. Exactly one runs three.

That is the number worth sitting with. Whatever split you pick, these programs assume you are training most days of the week, often with a paid trainer, a controlled kitchen, and a schedule built around recovery. The split is the easy part to copy. The frequency is the part that quietly decides whether copying works.

What to take from it

If you train three or four days a week, the honest match is upper/lower or full body — not the six-day bro split from a physique you admire, run at half the frequency it was designed for. Six of these programs are upper/lower, eight are full body, and those are the ones that survive being scaled down.
